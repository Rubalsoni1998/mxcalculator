About the Tax Bracket Calculator

The United States taxes income in layers. Each layer, called a bracket, has its own rate, and only the dollars that fall inside a bracket are taxed at that bracket's rate. Moving into a higher bracket never makes your whole income taxed at the higher rate. This calculator shows exactly how your income is split across the seven federal brackets (10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%) and what you owe in each.

You can start from gross income and let the calculator apply the standard deduction, or enter taxable income straight from your return. It reports your marginal rate (the rate on your next dollar), your effective rate (total tax divided by income) and how much more you can earn before reaching the next bracket. Updated November 21, 2026.

How Federal Income Tax Is Calculated

Taxable Income = Gross Income − Pre-tax Deductions − Standard (or Itemized) Deduction
Tax = Σ (income in each bracket × that bracket's rate)

Example (2026, single): You earn $85,000 and take the $16,100 standard deduction, so taxable income is $68,900.

The first $12,400 is taxed at 10% ($1,240). The next $38,000 (up to $50,400) is taxed at 12% ($4,560). The remaining $18,500 is taxed at 22% ($4,070). Total federal tax is about $9,870, an effective rate of 14.3% of taxable income, even though your top bracket is 22%.

Marginal vs. Effective Tax Rate

  • Marginal rate: The rate on your last dollar of taxable income. It tells you the tax on a raise, bonus or extra deduction.
  • Effective rate: Your total tax divided by your income. It is always lower than your marginal rate and is the best measure of your overall burden.

What This Calculator Leaves Out

  • Social Security and Medicare (FICA) tax, self-employment tax and the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax.
  • Tax credits such as the Child Tax Credit or Earned Income Credit. Credits reduce tax after the brackets are applied.
  • Lower rates on long-term capital gains and qualified dividends. See the Capital Gains Tax Calculator.
  • The senior deduction of up to $6,000 per eligible person (2025–2028), the qualified business income deduction and state or local taxes.

Sources: IRS tax inflation adjustments for tax year 2026 and Rev. Proc. 2025-32.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the 2026 federal tax brackets?

There are seven rates: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. For a single filer in 2026 the 10% bracket ends at $12,400 of taxable income and the 37% rate starts above $640,600. Married couples filing jointly reach 37% above $768,700.

If I move into a higher bracket, is all my income taxed at that rate?

No. Only the income inside the higher bracket is taxed at the higher rate. A raise can never make your take-home pay smaller because of brackets alone.

What is the 2026 standard deduction?

It is $16,100 for single filers and married filing separately, $24,150 for heads of household and $32,200 for married couples filing jointly. People who are 65 or older or blind get an extra $1,650 (married) or $2,050 (unmarried).

What is the difference between marginal and effective tax rate?

Your marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar of income. Your effective rate is your total tax divided by your income, which is lower because your first dollars are taxed in the cheaper brackets.

Which tax year should I use?

The return you file in 2026 covers tax year 2025, and the return you file in 2027 covers tax year 2026. Choose 2026 to plan this year's income and withholding, and 2025 to check the return you are filing now.

Does this include state income tax or FICA?

No. It estimates federal income tax only. Social Security and Medicare tax, self-employment tax and state taxes are separate.
