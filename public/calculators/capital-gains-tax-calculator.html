About the Capital Gains Tax Calculator

A capital gain is the profit you make when you sell an asset for more than your cost basis. Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, cryptocurrency, rental property and land are common examples. The IRS taxes the gain at different rates depending on how long you held the asset and how much other income you have. This calculator estimates the federal tax, the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) and an optional state tax, then shows your profit after tax. Updated November 21, 2026.

Short-Term vs. Long-Term Gains

  • Short-term (held one year or less): taxed as ordinary income at your regular 10%–37% rate.
  • Long-term (held more than one year): taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%, which are usually much lower.

Gain = Sale Price − Cost Basis − Selling Costs
Tax = Gain × applicable rate (stacked on top of your other taxable income)

2026 Long-Term Capital Gains Rates

The rate depends on your total taxable income. The gain is stacked on top of your other income, so part of one sale can be taxed at two different rates. Figures are from IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32.

Example: A single filer has $80,000 of other taxable income and sells shares held for three years at a $20,000 gain.

Their income is already above the $49,450 zero-rate line, so the entire gain is taxed at 15%: $20,000 × 15% = $3,000. If the same shares were held under a year, the gain would be taxed at their 22% to 24% ordinary rate instead.

Net Investment Income Tax (3.8%)

High earners owe an extra 3.8% on the lesser of their net investment income or the amount their modified adjusted gross income exceeds a fixed threshold: $200,000 for single and head of household, $250,000 for married filing jointly and $125,000 for married filing separately. These thresholds are set by law and are not adjusted for inflation.

Ways to Reduce Capital Gains Tax

  • Hold for more than a year so the gain qualifies for long-term rates.
  • Use the 0% bracket in a low-income year to sell appreciated assets tax-free at the federal level.
  • Harvest losses. Losses offset gains, and up to $3,000 of extra loss ($1,500 if married filing separately) can offset ordinary income each year, with the rest carried forward.
  • Add to your basis. Include purchase fees, reinvested dividends and improvements to real estate.
  • Use tax-advantaged accounts such as a 401(k), IRA or Roth IRA, where trades inside the account do not create capital gains tax.
  • Home sales: Many homeowners can exclude up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 for couples) on a primary residence they have owned and lived in for two of the last five years.

Sources: IRS Topic 409, Capital gains and losses and IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the 2026 long-term capital gains tax rates?

The rates are 0%, 15% and 20%. A single filer pays 0% on gains up to $49,450 of taxable income, 15% up to $545,500 and 20% above that. For married couples filing jointly the limits are $98,900 and $613,700.

How are short-term capital gains taxed?

Short-term gains on assets held one year or less are added to your income and taxed at your ordinary rate, from 10% to 37%.

What is cost basis?

Cost basis is what you paid for the asset, including fees, plus certain costs such as improvements to property. Your gain is the sale price minus basis and selling costs.

Do I pay capital gains tax on cryptocurrency?

Yes. The IRS treats crypto as property. Selling or trading it for a profit creates a short-term or long-term capital gain based on how long you held it. Use the cost you paid as basis.

What is the Net Investment Income Tax?

It is an extra 3.8% tax on investment income for people whose modified adjusted gross income is above $200,000 (single), $250,000 (married filing jointly) or $125,000 (married filing separately). It applies on top of the regular capital gains rate.

What if I sold at a loss?

A loss first cancels out capital gains. If you still have a net loss, you can deduct up to $3,000 against other income each year and carry the rest forward to later years.
