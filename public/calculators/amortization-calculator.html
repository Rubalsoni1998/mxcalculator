About the Amortization Calculator

Amortization is the process of paying off a loan with equal installments over time. Each payment covers interest first, with the rest reducing principal, so the share going to principal grows as the balance falls.

This calculator builds a complete amortization schedule from your loan amount, interest rate and term, and shows total interest, payoff date and the effect of extra payments.

How to Use the Amortization Calculator

  1. Enter the loan amount, interest rate and term.
  2. Pick the start date and add any extra payment.
  3. Click Calculate to see the schedule and totals.

Amortization Formula

Payment = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ). Each month, interest = balance × r and principal = payment − interest.

Example: A $250,000 loan at 6 percent for 30 years has a payment of $1,498.88 and total interest of about $289,600. In the first month about $1,250 of the payment is interest.

Why Early Payments Are Mostly Interest

Because interest is calculated on the outstanding balance, the early payments of a long loan are mostly interest. Paying extra principal early has the biggest effect on total interest.

Worked Example: Adding $200 a Month

Example: On the same loan, paying $200 extra principal each month cuts total interest from about $289,595 to about $203,363, a saving of roughly $86,233. The loan is paid off in 267 months instead of 360, which is 22 years and 3 months, about 7 years and 9 months early.

The savings are large because every extra dollar removes interest that would have been charged on it for the rest of the loan. The same $200 sent in year 20 saves far less than it does in year 1.

15-Year vs. 30-Year Terms

Shortening the term raises the payment but cuts the interest sharply. On $250,000 at 6 percent, a 30-year loan costs $1,498.88 a month and $289,595 in total interest. A 15-year loan costs $2,109.64 a month and $129,736 in interest. Lenders often quote a lower rate on shorter terms, which widens the gap further. You can compare scenarios with the mortgage calculator or test other loan types with the loan calculator.

When to Use a Schedule

A schedule is most useful when you are deciding whether to prepay, comparing two loan offers with different terms, or estimating how much equity you have built. Equity is the home value minus the balance, so the balance column shows how fast you are building it. Early in a 30-year loan that pace is slow, which matters if you may sell within a few years and want to know how much you would walk away with after paying off the lender.

Common Mistakes

  • Not telling the lender how to apply extra money. Many servicers treat an unmarked extra payment as an early payment of next month. Specify that it goes to principal.
  • Forgetting escrow. The schedule covers principal and interest only. Property tax, insurance and PMI are billed on top and can change each year.
  • Expecting a penny-perfect match. Servicers differ in rounding and in how they count days, so your statement can differ from the schedule by a few cents.
  • Assuming a fixed rate. For an adjustable-rate loan, the schedule is only valid until the next rate reset.
  • Ignoring prepayment penalties. Some loans, especially non-mortgage ones, charge a fee for early payoff. Check your note before paying extra.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an amortization schedule?

A table listing every payment with the interest, principal and remaining balance.

How do extra payments change amortization?

They lower the balance faster, reducing interest and shortening the loan.

Does amortization apply to car loans?

Yes. Most installment loans, including auto, student and personal loans, are amortized.

Why does my balance fall slowly at first?

Because most of each early payment covers interest.

How do I calculate the remaining balance on a loan?

Read the balance column of the schedule at the payment number you want. To do it by hand, subtract the principal part of each payment (payment minus balance times the monthly rate) from the starting balance, one month at a time. Your lender's payoff quote is the official figure.

Why does my balance barely drop in the first years of a mortgage?

Interest is charged on the full outstanding balance, so early payments are mostly interest. On a $250,000 loan at 6 percent over 30 years, only about $249 of the first $1,499 payment reduces principal. The principal share grows each month as the balance falls.

Is it better to make extra payments or refinance?

It depends on closing costs and the rate you could get. Extra payments cost nothing and shorten the loan at your current rate. Refinancing only helps if the savings from a lower rate outweigh fees and the longer time you may restart the term.

What is negative amortization?

Negative amortization happens when your payment is smaller than the interest due, so the unpaid interest is added to the balance and the loan grows instead of shrinking. It can occur with certain adjustable or payment-option loans. A standard fixed-rate schedule never does this.
