About the Dividend Calculator

A dividend is a share of a company's profit paid to its shareholders, usually in cash every month, quarter or year. Dividend investing is a popular way to build a stream of passive income, and the real power comes from reinvesting those payments so they buy more shares, which then pay more dividends of their own.

This calculator projects how an investment in a dividend stock, ETF or fund could grow over time. It accounts for your starting amount, monthly contributions, the starting dividend yield, yearly dividend increases, share price growth, payout frequency, dividend taxes and whether you reinvest. You get your final portfolio value, total dividends received, the annual dividend income you could be earning in the last year, and your yield on cost.

How the Calculation Works

The model runs month by month. It treats your holding as shares priced at $100 at the start, so a 3.5% yield means $3.50 of dividends per share per year.

Price(month) = Price(previous) × (1 + price growth)1/12
Dividend per share (year y) = Start dividend × (1 + dividend growth)y
Dividend payment = Shares × Dividend per share ÷ payouts per year
After-tax dividend = Payment × (1 − tax rate)

Each month your contribution buys shares at the current price. On payout months the after-tax dividend either buys more shares (DRIP on) or is collected as cash (DRIP off). Portfolio value is shares times price, plus any cash dividends collected.

Example: You invest $10,000 and add $200 a month into a stock yielding 3.5%. The dividend grows 5% a year, the share price grows 6% a year, dividends are paid quarterly and reinvested, and you pay 15% tax on dividends.

Over 20 years, your total contributions are $58,000. Because the dividends keep buying shares, the final portfolio is far larger than the amount you put in, and the yearly dividend income at the end is many times the first year's.

Key Terms

  • Dividend yield: Annual dividend per share divided by the current share price. A $2 dividend on a $50 stock is a 4% yield.
  • Dividend growth rate: The yearly percentage increase in the dividend per share. Companies with long records of raises are often called dividend growth stocks.
  • DRIP (dividend reinvestment plan): Automatically using dividends to buy more shares, usually without a commission.
  • Yield on cost: Your current annual dividend income divided by what you have invested in total. It rises over time when dividends grow.
  • Qualified dividends: Dividends that meet IRS holding-period rules and are taxed at lower long-term capital gains rates rather than ordinary income rates.

Tips for Dividend Investors

  • Do not chase the highest yield. A very high yield can mean the market expects the dividend to be cut. Look at the payout ratio and earnings stability.
  • Favor growth in the dividend. A lower yield that grows 8% a year can overtake a higher flat yield within a decade.
  • Use tax-advantaged accounts. Holding dividend payers in an IRA or 401(k) can defer or remove the tax drag. Set the tax rate to 0 to see the difference.
  • Diversify. Spread across sectors and companies so one dividend cut does not hurt your income much.
  • Stay consistent. Regular contributions and reinvestment drive most of the long-term result.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate dividend income?

Multiply the number of shares you own by the dividend per share. Owning 500 shares that pay $0.60 per share each quarter gives $300 per quarter, or $1,200 per year, before taxes.

What is a good dividend yield?

Many investors consider 2% to 5% a healthy range. Yields far above that can signal risk. A good yield also depends on dividend growth, payout ratio and the strength of the company.

Is it better to reinvest dividends or take cash?

Reinvesting usually builds more wealth over the long term through compounding. Taking cash makes sense when you need the income now, such as in retirement. Use the DRIP option above to compare both outcomes.

How are dividends taxed in the U.S.?

Qualified dividends are generally taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% depending on your taxable income, while ordinary dividends are taxed at your regular income tax rate. High earners may also owe a 3.8% net investment income tax. Dividends inside an IRA or 401(k) are not taxed until withdrawal, or never in a Roth.

What is yield on cost?

Yield on cost compares your current yearly dividends to the total amount you invested. If you put in $20,000 and now receive $2,000 a year in dividends, your yield on cost is 10%, even if the stock's current yield is only 3%.

Can a company cut its dividend?

Yes. Dividends are not guaranteed. A company can reduce, suspend or eliminate its dividend if profits fall, so treat projected income as an estimate.
