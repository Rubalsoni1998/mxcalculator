About the Water Intake Calculator

How much water you need depends on your sex, body, activity and climate. This calculator starts from the adequate intake levels set by the U.S. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, then adds extra for exercise and hot weather. It splits the total into what to drink and what you get from food, and it spreads your goal across your waking hours.

How the Calculation Works

Total fluids = Base adequate intake + Exercise allowance + Climate allowance
Exercise allowance = 12 oz × (Exercise minutes ÷ 30)
Climate allowance = 16 oz in hot or humid weather
Fluids from drinks = Base drink amount + Exercise + Climate
Hourly goal = Fluids from drinks ÷ Hours awake

Example: A man's base adequate intake is 125 oz of total fluids, of which about 101 oz comes from drinks and the rest from food. If he exercises 45 minutes, add 12 × 1.5 = 18 oz.

His drink goal is 101 + 18 = 119 oz, about 14.9 cups or 3.5 liters. Total fluids including food are 143 oz. Over 16 waking hours that is about 7.4 oz an hour.

What the Experts Recommend

The National Academies set adequate intakes of 3.7 liters (125 ounces) of total water a day for men and 2.7 liters (91 ounces) for women. These cover water from all beverages and food, with about 80% coming from drinks and about 20% from food. Pregnant women are advised to get about 3.0 liters in total (about 10 cups from drinks) and breastfeeding women about 3.8 liters (about 13 cups from drinks).

These levels are for healthy, mostly sedentary adults in a temperate climate. Very active people in hot weather can need six liters or more. For exercise, the American Council on Exercise suggests 17 to 20 ounces two to three hours before, 7 to 10 ounces every 10 to 20 minutes during, and about 8 ounces within 30 minutes after.

Key Terms

  • Adequate intake (AI): The daily amount that healthy people typically consume and that is expected to cover needs when a precise requirement is not known.
  • Total water: Water from drinking water, other beverages and the moisture in food.
  • Electrolytes: Minerals such as sodium and potassium that balance fluids. Long, heavy sweating can call for more than plain water.
  • Hyponatremia: Dangerously low blood sodium from drinking far more water than your body can excrete, most often in endurance events.

Tips for Staying Hydrated

  • Let thirst and urine color guide you. Pale yellow urine usually means good hydration, and dark urine can mean you need more.
  • Drink with meals and keep a bottle nearby. Small regular amounts beat catching up all at once.
  • Count other drinks. Milk, juice, tea and coffee all contribute, though go easy on sugary drinks.
  • Eat water-rich foods. Cucumbers, oranges, watermelon, strawberries and soups add fluid.
  • Drink more when you lose fluid. Fever, vomiting, diarrhea, hot weather and long workouts all raise needs.
  • Do not overdo it. More is not better. Drinking well beyond thirst can be harmful.

People with kidney, heart or liver conditions, or on medicines that affect fluids, should follow their doctor's fluid advice instead of these general numbers. This calculator is for adults 19 and over.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should I drink a day?

The U.S. National Academies set adequate total water intake at about 125 ounces (3.7 liters) for men and 91 ounces (2.7 liters) for women. That includes water from food and all beverages, so about 101 ounces of drinks for men and 74 ounces for women.

Does coffee or tea count toward my water intake?

Yes. Beverages containing caffeine contribute to your daily fluid total. The Institute of Medicine found no convincing evidence that caffeine causes a cumulative water deficit.

How much water should I drink when exercising?

The American Council on Exercise suggests 17 to 20 ounces two to three hours before, 7 to 10 ounces every 10 to 20 minutes during, and about 8 ounces afterward. This calculator also adds 12 ounces per 30 minutes of exercise to your daily total.

How much water should I drink if I am pregnant or breastfeeding?

The Institute of Medicine suggests about 10 cups of beverages a day during pregnancy and about 13 cups while breastfeeding. Ask your doctor, since needs vary.

Is the half your body weight in ounces rule correct?

It is a popular rule of thumb, not a medical standard. It gives a similar result for many adults, but the National Academies levels are based on measured intakes of healthy people. Use whichever you prefer as a starting point and adjust for thirst.

What are signs of dehydration?

Dark yellow urine, strong thirst, dry mouth, headache, tiredness and dizziness. Severe symptoms such as confusion, a very fast heartbeat or no urination need medical care.

Can you drink too much water?

Yes. Drinking much more than your body can excrete can dilute blood sodium, a condition called hyponatremia. It is rare but serious, and it is mostly seen in endurance events. Sipping steadily and following thirst is safer.

How many 16.9 oz water bottles is that?

Divide your drink goal in ounces by 16.9. For example, 101 ounces is about 6 bottles. The calculator shows the bottle count for your goal.
