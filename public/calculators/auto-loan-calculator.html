About the Auto Loan Calculator

An auto loan lets you buy a car and repay the cost in fixed monthly installments. The payment depends on the price, your down payment or trade-in, the interest rate and the term.

This auto loan calculator includes sales tax, fees and trade-in value so you can see the real monthly payment and total cost.

How to Use the Auto Loan Calculator

  1. Enter the vehicle price and your down payment and trade-in value.
  2. Enter the loan term, interest rate, sales tax and fees.
  3. Click Calculate to see the monthly payment and total interest.

Car Payment Formula

Payment = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ), with P the amount financed, r the monthly interest rate and n the number of months.

Example: A $30,000 car with $5,000 down finances $25,000. At 6 percent for 60 months the payment is $483.32 and total interest is about $3,999.

The 20/4/10 Guideline

A common rule of thumb: put 20 percent down, finance for no more than 4 years and keep total car costs (payment, insurance, fuel) under 10 percent of gross income.

How Loan Length Changes the Cost

A longer term lowers the monthly payment but raises the total interest, and it keeps you owing money on a depreciating car for longer. Here is a $25,000 loan at 6 percent at different terms:

Going from 60 to 84 months saves about $118 a month but adds roughly $1,679 in interest. Dealers often steer buyers toward the lowest payment, so compare the total cost, not just the monthly figure.

Worked Example: Price to Amount Financed

Example: A $32,000 car with 6 percent sales tax and $500 in fees costs $33,920 plus $500, or $34,420. Subtract $6,000 from a down payment and trade-in and you finance $28,420. At 7 percent for 60 months the payment is about $562.75, and total interest is about $5,345.

Sales tax rules vary by state. Some states tax the full price, while others tax only the price minus your trade-in value, so check yours with the car sales tax calculator. Registration, title and documentation fees also differ by state and dealer.

Why the Rate Matters

On a $28,000 loan over 60 months, a 4.9 percent rate gives a payment of $527.11 and about $3,627 in interest. At 7.9 percent the payment is $566.40 and interest is about $5,984, a difference of roughly $2,357. Your credit history, the loan term, the vehicle's age and the down payment all influence the rate you are offered. Getting a quote from a bank or credit union before you visit a dealer gives you a number to compare against dealer financing.

Trade-in and down payment

Every dollar of down payment or trade-in equity reduces the amount financed and the interest on it. A larger down payment also gives you a cushion against depreciation, since new cars typically lose value fastest in the first years. If your trade-in is worth less than the payoff on your old loan, enter only the positive equity in the calculator, and add any shortfall to the price you are financing.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Rolling negative equity into a new loan. If you owe more than your trade-in is worth, the difference is added to the new balance and you pay interest on it.
  • Confusing the interest rate and APR. APR can include certain finance charges. Use the rate in the calculator, and ask for the APR in writing.
  • Skipping add-ons review. Extended warranties, GAP coverage and similar products are often financed into the loan. Decide whether you want them, and know what they add to the balance.
  • Ignoring running costs. Insurance, fuel and maintenance are not part of the loan payment. Test your budget with the car affordability calculator.
  • Not checking the real rate. If you only know the payment, the interest rate calculator can reveal the rate you are being charged.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I put down on a car?

About 20 percent for a new car and 10 percent for a used one helps avoid owing more than the car is worth.

Is a longer loan term better?

It lowers the payment but increases total interest and the risk of negative equity.

Does trade-in reduce sales tax?

In many states, yes, but rules vary.

What credit score gets the best rate?

Scores above about 720 usually qualify for the lowest rates.

Should I get preapproved for a car loan before going to a dealer?

It is a good idea. A preapproval from a bank or credit union shows the rate and amount you qualify for, gives you a benchmark to compare against dealer financing, and lets you negotiate the price of the car separately from the financing.

Can I pay off a car loan early?

Usually yes. Most auto loans let you pay extra or pay off the balance early, but check your contract for a prepayment penalty. Also confirm that extra money is applied to principal rather than held as an advance on future payments.

How much should I put down on a car?

A common guideline is 20 percent for a new car and at least 10 percent for a used one. A larger down payment lowers the amount financed and helps you avoid owing more than the car is worth early in the loan.

Does a longer auto loan hurt me?

It lowers the monthly payment but increases total interest and the time you spend owing more than the car is worth. A 7-year loan on a $25,000 balance at 6 percent costs about $1,679 more in interest than a 5-year loan.
