About the Net Worth Calculator

Net worth is the single number that sums up your financial position: everything you own minus everything you owe. It is the most useful way to track progress over time, because a rising net worth means you are building wealth, whether the gain comes from saving more, paying down debt or from your investments and home growing in value.

This calculator adds up nine kinds of assets and six kinds of debt, then shows your net worth, your liquid net worth, home equity, your debt-to-asset ratio and a chart of where your assets sit. If you pick an age group, it also compares you with the latest Federal Reserve data on US households.

How the Calculation Works

Total assets = Cash + Savings + Investments + Retirement + Home + Other real estate + Vehicles + Business + Other
Total liabilities = Mortgage + HELOC + Auto + Student loans + Credit cards + Other debt
Net worth = Total assets − Total liabilities
Liquid net worth = Cash + Savings + Brokerage − Credit card debt
Home equity = Home value − Mortgage − HELOC
Debt-to-asset ratio = Total liabilities ÷ Total assets

Example: The sample figures in the form give total assets of $553,000 (including a $380,000 home and $90,000 in retirement accounts) and total liabilities of $300,000 (a $270,000 mortgage plus car, student and card debt).

Net worth is $553,000 − $300,000 = $253,000. For a household headed by someone aged 35 to 44, that is about 1.6 times the Federal Reserve median of $155,600.

US Household Net Worth by Age

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances is published every three years. The 2025 survey, released in October 2026, found a median US family net worth of $215,900 and an average of about $1.24 million. The median is the midpoint (half of households have more, half have less). The average is pulled up by a small number of very wealthy households, so the median is the better benchmark for a typical household.

Key Terms

  • Assets: Things you own that have value: cash, investments, retirement accounts, real estate, vehicles and a business.
  • Liabilities: Money you owe: mortgages, car and student loans, credit cards and other debts.
  • Liquid net worth: The part of your wealth you could turn into cash quickly. Your house does not count, which is why it is possible to be wealthy on paper and short on cash.
  • Home equity: The share of your home you actually own: market value minus what you still owe on it.
  • Debt-to-asset ratio: How much of what you own is financed with borrowed money. Lower is safer.

Tips for Counting and Growing Net Worth

  • Use realistic values. Count a car or a home at what it would sell for today, not what you paid. Do not include everyday household items.
  • Remember taxes on retirement money. Traditional 401(k) and IRA balances are before income tax, so spendable value is lower than the balance. Roth balances are generally tax-free in retirement.
  • Update on a schedule. Recalculate once or twice a year on the same date. The trend matters more than any single number.
  • Attack high-interest debt first. Paying off a credit card charging 20% or more improves net worth as surely as earning the same return on an investment.
  • Automate saving. Payroll deductions into a 401(k) and automatic transfers to savings build wealth without relying on willpower.
  • Do not panic over dips. Net worth moves with markets and home prices. Judge it over years, not weeks.

Negative net worth is common early in life, especially with student loans or a new mortgage, and it can turn positive quickly as debts are paid and savings grow.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate my net worth?

Add up everything you own (cash, investments, retirement accounts, home, vehicles and other valuables) and subtract everything you owe (mortgage, car loans, student loans, credit cards and other debts). The result is your net worth.

What is a good net worth by age?

The Federal Reserve 2025 survey shows a median of $33,000 for households under 35, $155,600 at 35 to 44, $253,700 at 45 to 54, $411,900 at 55 to 64, $431,300 at 65 to 74 and $504,900 at 75 and older. Being above the median for your age is a reasonable benchmark, but your goals matter more than the average.

Should I include my house in net worth?

Yes. Include the market value of your home as an asset and your mortgage and home equity loans as liabilities. The difference is your home equity. For a view of money you can reach quickly, use the liquid net worth figure, which leaves the house out.

Does net worth include retirement accounts?

Yes. Balances in 401(k)s, IRAs, Roth IRAs and similar accounts are assets. Keep in mind that withdrawals from traditional accounts are taxed as income, so the after-tax value is lower.

Is it bad to have a negative net worth?

Not necessarily. Many young adults have negative net worth because of student loans, a car loan or a new mortgage. What matters is the direction: paying down debt and adding savings moves it up over time.

What is the difference between median and average net worth?

The median is the middle value, with half of households above and half below. The average adds up all net worth and divides by the number of households, so a few billionaires pull it up. The 2025 US median was $215,900 and the average was about $1.24 million.

How often should I calculate my net worth?

Once or twice a year is enough for most people, such as each January or on your birthday. Use the same method each time so you can compare results fairly.

Is net worth the same as income?

No. Income is what you earn over a period. Net worth is what you have accumulated: assets minus debts at a point in time. A high earner who spends everything can have a low net worth, and a modest earner who saves steadily can have a high one.
