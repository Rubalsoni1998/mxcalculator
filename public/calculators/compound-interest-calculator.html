About the Compound Interest Calculator

Compound interest is interest earned on both your original money and the interest it has already earned. Over long periods it makes savings grow faster than simple interest, which is why starting early matters.

This calculator shows how an initial deposit and regular contributions grow over time at your chosen rate and compounding frequency.

How to Use the Compound Interest Calculator

  1. Enter the starting amount and annual interest rate.
  2. Choose the time period and how often interest compounds.
  3. Add any regular contribution, then click Calculate.

Compound Interest Formula

A = P × (1 + r ÷ n)^(n × t), where P is the principal, r the annual rate, n the compounding periods per year and t the number of years.

Example: $10,000 at 5 percent compounded annually for 10 years grows to $16,288.95, earning $6,288.95 in interest. With simple interest it would earn only $5,000.

The Rule of 72

Divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate how many years it takes to double your money. At 6 percent it takes about 12 years; at 8 percent about 9 years.

How Compounding Frequency Changes the Result

The more often interest is added to the balance, the more it earns on itself. The effect is real but modest. Here is $10,000 at 5 percent for 10 years:

Annually$16,288.955.000%
Quarterly$16,436.195.095%

Daily$16,486.655.127%

Going from annual to daily compounding adds under $200 over a decade. The rate and the time horizon matter far more than the compounding schedule. The effective annual yield (often called APY on savings accounts) is the figure to compare between banks, because it already includes compounding.

Why Time Beats Almost Everything Else

Because growth builds on growth, the same deposit gains more in each later year. With no new deposits, $10,000 at 5 percent compounded annually becomes $16,288.95 after 10 years, $26,532.98 after 20 years and $43,219.42 after 30 years. The second decade adds about $10,244, and the third adds about $16,686.

Example: Suppose two people each save $200 a month and earn a hypothetical 7 percent, compounded monthly. One starts at 25 and stops at 65, investing $96,000 in total, and ends with about $524,963. The other starts at 35 and invests $72,000, ending with about $243,994. Ten extra years of contributions more than doubles the result.

The 7 percent figure is an assumption, not a promise. Actual investment returns vary from year to year and can be negative, unlike a fixed-rate savings account. To model regular contributions alongside a lump sum, try the investment calculator.

What the Calculator Does Not Include

  • Taxes. Interest in a regular savings or brokerage account is generally taxable in the year it is earned, which slows compounding. Tax-advantaged accounts work differently. See the IRS for current rules.
  • Inflation. A balance of $43,219 in 30 years buys less than $43,219 does today. Use the inflation calculator to convert results into today's dollars.
  • Fees. Account and fund fees reduce your effective return. A one-point fee on a 7 percent return is a 6 percent return in practice.
  • Variable rates. Savings account rates change. The calculator assumes one fixed rate for the whole period.

Compound Interest Works Against Borrowers Too

The same math applies to debts. Credit card balances typically compound daily or monthly, so unpaid interest is added to what you owe and then charged interest itself. If you only need a quick comparison of simple and compound growth, the interest calculator shows both side by side.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is only on the principal. Compound interest also earns interest on previous interest.

Does compounding frequency matter?

Yes, but only a little. Daily compounding earns slightly more than monthly, and monthly more than annual.

How can I make compound interest work for me?

Start early, contribute regularly and leave earnings invested.

What is APY?

Annual percentage yield is the effective annual rate after compounding.

What is the difference between APR and APY?

APR is the stated annual rate without compounding, while APY includes the effect of compounding within the year. A 5 percent rate compounded monthly has an APY of about 5.12 percent. When comparing savings accounts, APY is the better number to use.

How do I calculate compound interest with monthly deposits?

Add the future value of each deposit to the future value of your starting amount. The deposit formula is payment times ((1 + r) to the n, minus 1), divided by r, with r the rate per period. The calculator does this when you enter a regular contribution.

Is compound interest taxable?

In most regular savings, CD and brokerage accounts, interest is taxable income in the year it is credited, even if you leave it in the account. Retirement accounts can defer or exempt that tax depending on type. Check IRS guidance or ask a tax professional.

How long does it take to double money at 7 percent?

About 10 years. The Rule of 72 gives 72 divided by 7, or roughly 10.3 years, and the exact figure with annual compounding is about 10.24 years. This assumes the rate holds steady and no money is withdrawn.
