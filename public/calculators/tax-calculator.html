About the Federal Income Tax Calculator

The U.S. federal income tax is progressive: your income is divided into bands, and each band is taxed at its own rate. That means only the part of income inside a higher band is taxed at the higher rate.

This calculator applies the IRS brackets for your filing status and tax year, subtracts the standard or itemized deduction, and shows your estimated tax, marginal rate and effective rate.

How to Use the Federal Income Tax Calculator

  1. Select the tax year and your filing status.
  2. Enter your gross annual income.
  3. Choose standard or itemized deduction.
  4. Click Calculate to see taxable income, tax owed and rates.

How Federal Income Tax Is Calculated

Taxable income = gross income − deduction. Tax is the sum of each bracket's taxable amount times its rate. Effective rate = total tax ÷ gross income.

Example: Illustration: with brackets of 10% to $10,000 and 20% above, taxable income of $30,000 owes $1,000 + $4,000 = $5,000, an effective rate of 16.7 percent, even though the marginal rate is 20 percent.

Marginal vs Effective Tax Rate

The marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar of income. The effective rate is your total tax divided by total income and is always lower. Moving into a higher bracket never reduces your take-home pay on the income you already earned.

A Worked Example With Illustrative Brackets

The numbers below are made up to show the method, not real IRS brackets. Assume 10 percent applies up to $12,000, 12 percent from $12,000 to $48,000, and 22 percent above $48,000, and that your taxable income is $70,000.

  • 10% × $12,000 = $1,200
  • 12% × $36,000 = $4,320
  • 22% × $22,000 = $4,840

Total tax is $10,360. Your marginal rate is 22 percent, but your effective rate on taxable income is only 14.8 percent. Earning $1,000 more would add $220 of tax, not $10,360 × 22 percent.

Deductions Versus Credits

A deduction lowers taxable income, so its value depends on your bracket. A $1,000 deduction at a 22 percent marginal rate saves $220. A credit reduces the tax itself, dollar for dollar, so a $1,000 credit saves $1,000. Some credits are refundable, meaning they can produce a refund even if you owe no tax. To see how two popular credits work, try the child tax credit calculator and the EITC calculator.

Standard or Itemized Deduction

Most taxpayers take the standard deduction, which is a fixed amount set each year by filing status. You itemize only if your qualifying expenses, such as mortgage interest, state and local taxes up to the legal cap, and charitable gifts, add up to more. The amounts and brackets change every year, so use the tax year selector and confirm figures on IRS.gov.

What This Estimate Leaves Out

  • Payroll taxes: Social Security and Medicare tax are separate from income tax.
  • State and local tax: Most states have their own income tax; some have none.
  • Special rates: Long-term capital gains and qualified dividends use different rates. See the capital gains tax calculator.
  • Adjustments and other taxes: Retirement contributions, student loan interest, self-employment tax and the alternative minimum tax can all change the final number.

Withholding Is Not Your Final Bill

Your employer withholds tax from each paycheck based on your Form W-4. When you file, your actual tax is compared with what was withheld. If more was withheld, you get a refund; if less, you owe the difference. A large refund simply means you lent money to the government interest-free during the year. The tax refund estimator helps compare withholding against your calculated tax. For personal situations, a licensed tax professional or the IRS can give definitive answers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the standard deduction?

A fixed amount you subtract from income without listing expenses. It depends on filing status and year.

Should I itemize?

Itemize only when your deductible expenses, such as mortgage interest and charity, exceed the standard deduction.

Does this include state tax or FICA?

No. It estimates federal income tax only.

Will I owe this exact amount?

No. Credits, other income and withholding change what you owe.

What is the difference between gross income and taxable income?

Gross income is everything you earn before adjustments. Taxable income is what remains after subtracting adjustments and either the standard or itemized deduction. Income tax brackets apply to taxable income, not gross income.

Do I pay the higher rate on all my income if I move up a bracket?

No. Only the portion of taxable income above the bracket threshold is taxed at the higher rate. Income below it is still taxed at the lower rates, so a raise never reduces your pay on income you already earned.

How is the effective tax rate different from my bracket?

Your bracket is the marginal rate on your last dollar of income. Your effective rate is total tax divided by income, and it is lower because lower brackets apply to earlier dollars. It better describes your overall tax burden.

Where do I find the current IRS tax brackets?

The IRS publishes brackets, standard deduction amounts and other limits each year on irs.gov, usually announced in the fall for the following tax year. Search for the annual inflation adjustments or the instructions for Form 1040.
