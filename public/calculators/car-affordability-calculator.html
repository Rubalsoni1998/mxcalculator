About the Car Affordability Calculator

The car you can afford depends on more than the monthly payment. The interest rate, the loan term, your down payment, any trade-in, sales tax and the cost of insurance and fuel all matter. This calculator starts from your income and budget, applies the current average auto loan rates, and works backward to the most you can spend on a vehicle. It also checks your plan against the popular 20/4/10 rule and a debt-to-income limit. Updated November 21, 2026.

How Car Affordability Is Calculated

Loan Amount = Monthly Payment × [1 − (1 + r)−n] ÷ r where r is the monthly rate and n is the number of months
Max Price = (Loan + Down Payment + Trade-In Equity + Tax Credit on Trade-In − Fees) ÷ (1 + Sales Tax Rate)

Example: $75,000 income, 20/4/10 rule, $400 a month for insurance and fuel, 60-month loan at 6.35%, $5,000 down, 7% sales tax and $800 in fees.

Ten percent of $6,250 monthly income is $625. After $400 of running costs, $225 is left for the loan, which supports a loan of about $11,540. Adding the down payment and removing fees and tax gives a price near $14,700. The rule is strict, so many buyers choose the percent-of-income option instead.

The 20/4/10 Rule

  • 20% down on the purchase price, which helps avoid owing more than the car is worth.
  • 4 years (48 months) or less for the loan term, to limit interest.
  • 10% of gross income as a ceiling for all monthly car costs, including the payment, insurance, fuel and repairs.

It is a conservative guide. With new-car prices near $50,000 and average loan terms over 69 months, many buyers cannot meet all three parts, so this calculator also lets you size the loan by a percent of income or a payment you choose.

How to Afford More Car Without Overpaying

  • Improve your credit first. Moving from near prime to prime can cut a new-car rate from 9.71% to 6.15%.
  • Make a bigger down payment. It lowers the loan and the interest, and reduces the risk of negative equity.
  • Choose a shorter term. A 48-month loan costs far less interest than an 84-month loan at the same rate.
  • Get pre-approved. Comparing a bank, a credit union and the dealer's financing gives you leverage.
  • Consider a used car. Used loans are smaller, though the rates are higher.
  • Check the total cost. Insurance, fuel and maintenance can add several hundred dollars a month.

Sources: Experian State of the Automotive Finance Market, Q2 2026 (published November 13, 2026); Edmunds auto loan data, October 2026; Kelley Blue Book, September 2026.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much car can I afford on a $75,000 salary?

It depends on your rules. The 20/4/10 rule keeps all car costs near $625 a month, which supports a car of about $14,700 after running costs. If you size the loan at 12% of gross income, your payment would be $750 and the price could be about $40,000 with a 60-month loan.

What is the 20/4/10 rule for buying a car?

Put 20% down, finance for no more than four years, and keep total monthly car costs, including insurance and fuel, under 10% of your gross income.

What is the average car loan rate right now?

Experian reported averages of 6.35% for new cars and 11.19% for used cars in Q2 2026. Edmunds reported 7.3% for new and 11% for used in October 2026. Buyers with the best credit pay about 4.4% on a new car and those with the lowest scores pay about 16%.

How much of my income should go to a car payment?

A common guide is 10% to 15% of gross income for the loan payment, or 10% for all car costs. Lenders also check that your total debt, including housing, stays under about 36% to 45% of gross income.

Is a 72 or 84-month car loan a bad idea?

The payment is lower but you pay much more interest, and you are more likely to owe more than the car is worth. If you need more than 60 months to afford the car, it may be more car than your budget supports.

Should I include insurance in my car budget?

Yes. Insurance, fuel, repairs and registration are part of the real cost of owning a car, and the 20/4/10 rule counts them against your 10% limit.
